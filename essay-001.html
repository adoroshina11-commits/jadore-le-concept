The Diamond Was Never Just a Stone:
How an Industry Manufactured Rarity

For more than a century, the diamond industry sold us something larger than carbon. It sold rarity, permanence and the idea that love could be measured in carats. Lab-grown diamonds are forcing us to ask how much of that value belonged to the stone — and how much belonged to the story.

A diamond seems like the perfect luxury object. It is small, durable, visually spectacular and surrounded by a language of rarity. We are taught to understand it through carat, cut, clarity and colour, but behind those measurements sits another, less visible quality: belief.

For generations, consumers have been taught that a diamond is valuable because nature made very few of them and because acquiring one requires access to something exceptional. Yet the modern diamond market was never shaped by geology alone. Supply was organised, distribution was controlled, advertising attached diamonds to marriage and permanence, and an ordinary mineral became one of the most emotionally loaded luxury products in the world.

Now technology has complicated that story. A laboratory can produce a diamond with essentially the same chemical composition, crystal structure, optical properties and physical properties as one extracted from the earth. The difference is no longer simply what the object is. It is where it came from, how long it took to form and what we are willing to believe that origin is worth.

De Beers Diamond Trade · Historical Reference

The Old System

Rarity was never purely geological.

For much of the twentieth century, De Beers occupied an extraordinary position in the diamond market. It did not simply own mines. Through its own production, joint ventures, purchasing agreements and its Central Selling Organisation, the company controlled the route through which much of the world's rough-diamond supply reached buyers.

The European Commission later described De Beers as having controlled more than 80 percent of worldwide rough-diamond supply for much of the twentieth century. GIA's history of rough-diamond distribution similarly estimates that De Beers commanded roughly 75 to 80 percent of world rough-diamond sales through much of the last century.

That distinction matters. De Beers did not need to own 80 percent of every mine in order to exert enormous influence over the market. Control over distribution meant influence over how much rough entered the market, who could purchase it and how the industry's supply was organised.

De Beers Advertising · Historical Reference

Selling Permanence

Then the stone became a promise.

Controlling supply was only part of the equation. Diamonds also had to mean something.

In 1947, copywriter Frances Gerety wrote four words for the De Beers account at advertising agency N.W. Ayer: “A Diamond Is Forever.” The campaign connected the physical durability of a diamond with romantic permanence and helped reinforce the diamond engagement ring as a cultural expectation.

It was an extraordinary piece of luxury storytelling because the product became inseparable from the emotion attached to it. A diamond was no longer merely purchased because it sparkled. It represented commitment, status, adulthood and permanence.

The industry had transformed carbon into symbolism. That may ultimately have been its most valuable achievement.

Laboratory-Grown Diamond · Editorial Reference

The Disruption

What happens when we learn to make the stone?

A laboratory-grown diamond is not cubic zirconia and it is not moissanite. According to the Gemological Institute of America, laboratory-grown diamonds have essentially the same chemical composition, crystal structure, optical properties and physical properties as natural diamonds.

Their histories, however, could hardly be more different. Natural diamonds formed deep within the earth over immense geological periods. Laboratory diamonds can be grown using high-pressure, high-temperature technology or chemical vapour deposition. Modern production can create gem-quality stones in periods measured in days or weeks rather than geological time.

Suddenly, one of luxury's oldest equations becomes unstable. If two stones can look effectively identical to the unaided eye, but one can be produced repeatedly, what exactly is the consumer paying for when choosing the other?

Digital Diamond Market · Editorial Reference

From Counter to Screen

The buying experience changed too.

The traditional diamond purchase depended partly on information asymmetry. The jeweller understood the stone; the consumer relied on expertise, certification and trust. Today, that relationship is increasingly digital.

Consumers can sit at home and filter enormous inventories by carat, cut, colour, clarity, certification and price. Digital grading reports can be accessed instantly. Stones can be compared side by side before the buyer ever enters a boutique.

McKinsey has identified e-commerce as an increasingly important channel for fine jewellery, particularly among younger consumers. The effect is not simply convenience. Digital comparison makes pricing more visible. A product once surrounded by mystery begins to behave more like a transparent marketplace.

The diamond is still emotional. The process of buying one is becoming considerably less mysterious.

Contemporary Diamond Jewellery · Editorial Reference

A New Consumer

Bigger stone. Smaller mythology.

The most important change may not be technological at all. It may be generational.

The Knot's research found that lab-grown centre stones represented 52 percent of engagement rings among its 2024 respondents. Its 2026 Real Weddings Study, covering U.S. couples married in 2025, put that figure at 61 percent. In only a few years, what had been positioned as an alternative had moved into the mainstream of its surveyed market.

The same research found another revealing shift: couples were buying larger stones while average engagement-ring spending fell. Among its 2025 respondents, the average lab-grown centre stone was two carats, compared with 1.6 carats for natural stones.

This does not necessarily mean younger consumers no longer care about heritage or savoir-faire. It suggests something more complicated. They may not automatically accept heritage as sufficient justification for price.

Design, scale, personal meaning, transparency and accessibility can compete with provenance. The story still matters. The consumer is simply becoming more willing to choose which story deserves a premium.

Lightbox · De Beers Group · Lab-Grown Diamond Reference

Mass Production

Accessibility has a price of its own.

The success of laboratory-grown diamonds created the same problem that confronts almost every product that becomes easier to manufacture: abundance pushes against value.

In 2018, De Beers entered lab-grown jewellery itself through Lightbox, initially pricing its stones on a simple per-carat basis. Seven years later, the company announced that it intended to close the business. De Beers said wholesale lab-grown diamond prices had fallen 90 percent since Lightbox launched, as production expanded and competition intensified.

That collapse exposes the paradox at the centre of the lab-grown revolution. Accessibility is precisely what makes the product attractive to consumers, but accessibility also removes one of the foundations on which traditional luxury has historically been built: scarcity.

When almost anyone can produce more of the material, value must migrate somewhere else — toward design, brand, craftsmanship, provenance or emotion.

Diamond Mining · Supply Chain & Ethics Reference

The Ethics Problem

Neither side gets an easy moral victory.

Lab-grown diamonds are frequently presented as the ethical answer to mining, and the appeal is understandable. Mining industries can carry serious environmental and human-rights risks, making traceability and responsible sourcing increasingly important to consumers.

But “grown in a laboratory” should not automatically be read as “sustainable.” Diamond growth requires significant energy. The environmental impact of laboratory-grown diamonds can vary considerably depending on the technology used, the energy source powering production and the manufacturing practices behind the stone.

Mass production also introduces a different question. If laboratory diamonds are manufactured at enormous scale and sold at continuously declining prices, they begin to participate in the same cycle of abundance and consumption that luxury increasingly claims it wants to move beyond.

The future of ethical jewellery may therefore depend less on a simple choice between “mined” and “lab-grown” and more on transparency: where a stone came from, how it was produced, what energy was used, who benefited from its production and how honestly those facts are communicated.

After Rarity

So what is a diamond actually worth?

Perhaps that has always been the wrong question.

The history of diamonds demonstrates that luxury value has never existed entirely inside the object. De Beers understood this exceptionally well. Supply mattered, but so did distribution. Geology mattered, but so did advertising. A stone became powerful because an industry constructed an entire cultural language around it.

Lab-grown diamonds have not destroyed that language. They have exposed how it works.

If the physical characteristics of a diamond can be reproduced while its price falls dramatically, then natural-diamond producers can no longer rely on sparkle alone. They must sell geological time, provenance, traceability and rarity. Lab-grown producers face the opposite problem: if their stones become increasingly abundant, they must build value through design, branding, craftsmanship and meaning.

Both sides are therefore moving toward the same uncomfortable truth. Carbon was never enough.

The diamond industry spent a century teaching us that the stone was valuable because it was rare. Lab-grown diamonds are forcing it to confront a different possibility: perhaps the rarest thing was never the diamond. It was the story surrounding it.
